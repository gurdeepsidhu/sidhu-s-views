The Great Sunday Samosa Incident of the 90s

A sweet, hilarious memory of 1990s childhood: when there were no mobile phones, fast food wasn't a daily trend, and an unbothered brother forgot the samosas and innocently asked for lunch.

By Gurdeep Sidhu • September 20, 2026 • 14 min read • 90s Malgudi Nostalgia
Language / भाषा: English | 🇮🇳 यह लेख हिंदी में पढ़ें →

  3. Scene 3: The Grand Arrival & The Empty Hands (12:30 PM)
  4. Scene 4: Mother's Thunder & The Legendary Question

“In the golden 1990s, a Sunday morning in a middle-class Indian household was never a quiet, aesthetic brunch. It was a loud, high-stakes domestic epic—where sending someone to fetch piping-hot samosas from the local halwai was not an errand, but a sacred diplomatic mission equivalent to ratifying a United Nations treaty!”

Today, whenever anyone craves something savoury, they pull a glowing glass slab from their pocket, swipe two fingers across the screen, and within fourteen minutes, a helmeted delivery rider rings the doorbell with a brown paper parcel. There is zero anticipation, zero heartbreak, zero human drama. Just a cold, efficient digital transaction.

But back in the 1990s? Oh, the world was forged from an entirely different clay!

There were no smartphones glued to our palms, no algorithmic feeds demanding our outrage, and no instant gratification. Sunday mornings in our household began at eight o'clock with the warm, crackling strains of Doordarshan's Rangoli. And tuning into Doordarshan was an acrobatic military operation in itself. Every twenty minutes, someone had to scramble up to the rooftop terrace to twist the aluminium antenna, while someone in the courtyard yelled at the top of their lungs: "Did it come? Did it come? Turn it slightly left! No, stop, stop! Amitabh Bachchan's face looks like a swarm of mosquitoes! Okay, hold it right there!"

In those unhurried days, the domestic realm was governed by an absolute constitutional monarch: our mother. And Mother lived by an unshakeable household doctrine that even the Supreme Court could not overturn: "Outside food ruins your digestion! Plain home-cooked dal and roti is the only true wealth of human health!"

Consequently, whenever Sunday morning arrived and Mother unexpectedly declared that "Today, we shall fetch fresh samosas from the bazaar for breakfast!"—the house shook to its foundations. My sister and I would leap into the air as though we had just won the state lottery.

The Universal Law of 90s Indian Cravings: In those days, a halwai's samosa was not a mere snack. It was a golden, blistering triangular monument wrapped in recycled sheets of the morning Hindi newspaper, weeping glorious translucent rings of mustard oil. And the accompanying pouch of fiery green mint chutney was tied with a knot so devilishly tight that opening it required the surgical precision of human front teeth!

Scene 1: The Diplomatic Envoy & The Sacred Fifty-Rupee Note (9:00 AM)

The two hands of the grandfather clock met squarely at nine in the morning. In the kitchen, Mother had already placed a colossal brass saucepan on the burner. The fragrant perfume of crushed ginger, cracked green cardamom, and simmering tea leaves was wafting through every corridor, reducing my sister and me to drooling sentinels on the veranda.

Now arose the fateful question: Who would be dispatched to the bazaar?

By the unwritten laws of household hierarchy, this monumental mission fell squarely upon the slender shoulders of my thirteen-year-old brother, Raju.

Now, to send Raju out through the front gate on an errand was the exact emotional equivalent of launching a paper boat into the monsoon rapids of the Ganges. You knew it set sail, but where it would wash ashore—or if it would survive the voyage at all—lay entirely in the hands of the gods. Raju was not a wicked boy; he was simply possessed of a mind like an inquisitive squirrel. Sent to buy coriander, he would stop halfway to stroke a stray puppy; sent for milk, he would stand transfixed beneath a banyan tree for forty minutes watching a trapped kite flutter in the breeze.

Mother unknotted the heavy brass key tied to the corner of her saree pallu with customary solemnity. The steel Godrej almirah opened with its signature drawn-out groan: "Crrreeeak..." From its innermost locker, she extracted a crisp, immaculate fifty-rupee note.

In the 1990s, fifty rupees was not loose pocket shrapnel—it was a sovereign treasury bond! Mother folded the currency note twice, pulled Raju toward her by the elbow, and conducted an immediate border inspection of his half-pant pockets.

"Are there any holes in these pockets?" Mother demanded, turning the lining inside out.

Raju's pocket was an archaeological excavation site. Out tumbled: two iridescent glass marbles, a rusted bicycle valve, a flattened Campa Cola bottle cap, and a stump of stolen blackboard chalk. Mother confiscated these national treasures and shoved the fifty-rupee note into the deepest, safest recess of his shorts.

“Listen to me with both ears wide open, Raju. Ride straight to Munna Halwai in the main bazaar. Ask for eight scalding-hot samosas. Get the green mint chutney and the sweet tamarind saunth tied in separate pouches. Do not look to the left, do not look to the right, and be back on your bicycle in precisely ten minutes!”

Raju nodded with such grave solemnity that one would think the Prime Minister had just entrusted him with the nation's nuclear launch codes. He puffed out his chest, dragged out his colossal black Hero roadster bicycle—a machine forged from solid iron that outweighed Raju by at least ten kilograms, and possessed a chronic medical condition where its greasy chain slipped off the sprocket every four hundred metres.

Raju spun the loose dome of the bicycle bell, producing a triumphant 'trin-trin!' Standing at the threshold, my sister barked our final conditions: "Raju! Make sure they are fresh out of the kadhai! Don't bring cold ones! And hold the chutney bag upright so it doesn't burst!"

Raju flashed us a steely thumbs-up, stood on the pedals with all his might, and vanished down the lane in a romantic cloud of dry summer dust. At that moment, my sister and I truly believed that heaven was only fifteen minutes away.

Alas! Little did we realize that that cheerful thumbs-up was, in truth, our Sunday breakfast waving goodbye forever.

Scene 2: The Agonizing Two-Hour Wait & Forced Kitchen Duty (10:00 AM - 12:00 PM)

9:15 AM. My sister and I were perched on the cool red-oxide steps of the veranda like expectant sparrows.

"The halwai must be frying a fresh batch in his giant iron kadhai," I offered generously. "You know how it is—when the raw triangles hit the bubbling mustard oil, it takes at least ten minutes to turn golden-brown. He'll bring them piping hot!"

9:45 AM. Generous optimism was quietly replaced by a creeping, cold dread.

In the kitchen, the pot of ginger-cardamom tea had cooled down well past room temperature. Across its desolate surface had formed a thick, leathery, wrinkled blanket of malai that resembled the hide of an ancient rhinoceros. My sister poked it with a teaspoon and grimaced: "Look at this! Our morning tea has turned into cold pond water! And His Royal Highness has not even appeared on the horizon!"

10:30 AM. This was no longer an ordinary delay. This had evolved into the ultimate crucible of middle-class Indian patience.

Remember: this was 1995. There was no WhatsApp live location. There was no phone booth at the halwai's corner. Raju was entirely off the grid. All we possessed was an empty, sun-baked street and the boundless, terrifying architecture of family paranoia.

The Evolution of Household Conspiracies (10:00 AM to 11:30 AM):
• 10:15 AM: "His bicycle tyre must have suffered a puncture. Poor boy is probably dragging the heavy iron frame in the heat."
• 10:45 AM: "Did the fifty-rupee note slip from his pocket? Is he crawling on all fours along the bazaar road searching for it?"
• 11:15 AM: "No! That traitor is chasing a stray dog, or he spotted a severed kite drifting over the rooftops and sprinted after it!"
• 11:30 AM: "If he ate those samosas by himself on the roadside, he will not survive the afternoon!"

By eleven o'clock, our stomachs were staging a full-fledged wrestling tournament. Every distant metallic rattle—every faint 'trin!' of a passing cyclist—caused my sister and me to press our foreheads against the veranda window grill like condemned prisoners watching for a royal pardon. Every single time, it was merely the milkman or a vegetable hawker shouting "Aloo-gobhi le lo!"

By 11:45 AM, Mother's maternal calm had evaporated completely. She was pacing the corridor between the kitchen and the front gate like an army general inspecting a besieged garrison.

And then, at precisely 12:00 PM, Mother dealt the final, crushing blow that rubbed coarse salt directly into our famished wounds:

The Tragic Ultimatum of High Noon

"Both of you, stop daydreaming about samosas! It is twelve o'clock. Breakfast is dead. March into the kitchen this instant, peel the potatoes, and chop the onions for lunch!"

Good heavens! Talk about the cruelest cosmic irony!

Here we were, having spent three agonizing hours fantasizing about spiced, cumin-roasted potatoes wrapped inside crisp, buttery pastry... and now we were sentenced to hard labour on the kitchen floor, peeling muddy, raw potatoes and weeping bitter tears over pungent onions!

The onion fumes burned my eyes until tears cascaded down my cheeks. My sister was stabbing her paring knife into the potatoes with such ferocity that it was clear she was visualizing Raju's collarbone. We made a solemn pact over the chopping board: "Just let him step through that gate. We will pulverize him into chutney!"

Scene 3: The Grand Arrival & The Empty Hands (12:30 PM)

12:30 PM sharp.

The midday sun stood directly overhead. Inside the house, between the rhythmic hiss of the pressure cooker and the tearful residue of chopped onions, a tense, ominous silence hung in the air.

And then, drifting through the open courtyard gate, came a sound.

It was not the shuffle of a guilty boy. It was not the fearful whimper of a truant. It was—a jaunty, cheerful, completely carefree melodic whistle!

We dropped our knives, abandoned the onions, and bolted to the veranda. The iron gate creaked open. And there stood our long-lost prodigal brother.

Raju's entrance belonged in an epic military painting depicting the return of a battle-scarred warrior. His left trouser leg was hitched up past his knee. Both shins and elbows were coated in a thick, powdery layer of khaki soil, as though he had spent the morning ploughing a dry riverbed. Two buttons were missing from his shirt. His hair stood upright in wild tufts, resembling an unkempt sparrow's nest. Sweat poured down his temples, yet across his face was plastered the most serene, victorious smile imaginable.

In his right hand, he swung with immense pride: a veteran wooden cricket bat—its fractured spine held together by no fewer than twenty tightly wound layers of sticky yellow electrical insulation tape!

And in his left hand?

Every eye in the house locked onto his left hand.

Nothing. Absolute zero. Pure, unadulterated air!

The Majestic Absence: No brown paper parcel. No oily newspaper packet. No eight samosas. No green mint chutney. No sweet tamarind saunth. And most certainly no crisp fifty-rupee note! His left hand was empty of everything on earth—save for the deep red circular impression of a hard cricket ball stamped into his palm!

In that single, breathtaking second, the entire saga materialized before us with crystal clarity:

As Raju pedalled toward the bazaar, he had passed the dusty clearing beneath the ancient peepal tree. There, the neighbourhood boys were locked in a desperate gully cricket match against the dreaded Railway Colony eleven. Their team had lost four wickets for just twelve runs. As Raju's bicycle rattled past, the team captain had roared: "Raju bhai! Just one over, please! The colony's honour is at stake!"

And to any thirteen-year-old Indian boy in 1995, 'just one over' was a metaphysical portal into another dimension—a black hole where three and a half hours, fifty rupees, eight samosas, and a starving family vanished into the quantum ether!

The fifty-rupee note? It had either been deposited beneath a brick serving as a wicket, or sacrificed to the tall weeds during a heroic dive at mid-wicket.

My sister and I stepped onto the threshold, clutching our rolling pins and kitchen knives, glaring at him with eyes so wide with murderous fury that had human gaze possessed thermal properties, Raju and his taped willow would have dissolved into ash on the spot!

Scene 4: The Thunder of Mother & The Legendary Question

Yet the sisters were mere spectators. The true storm was about to make landfall from the kitchen!

Mother emerged through the doorway wielding a heavy wooden belan (rolling pin) like Queen Lakshmibai charging into battle. The sight of Raju's dust-caked knees, his battered bat, and his spectacularly empty hands caused Mother's complexion to turn from scarlet to deep, royal purple.

For the next ten uninterrupted minutes, our veranda bore witness to an oral epic that scholars of Indian motherhood would study for generations!

This was not a mundane reprimand. It was a grand, symphonic monologue that rolled like thunder across the courtyard:

"Raju! Where are the samosas?! Where is that fifty-rupee note?! Look at the clock—it is half past twelve! The entire household has been starving since nine in the morning! Your sisters have been crying over raw onions, while you were rolling in the bazaar dirt?! Do fifty-rupee notes grow on neem trees?! Last month you lost your new umbrella, you scored fourteen out of fifty in your mathematics exam, and now you have starved your family! You will achieve nothing in this life—you will end up repairing bicycle punctures outside the bus terminus!"

It was a masterclass in domestic rhetoric: geography, arithmetic, economics, family honour, and Raju's doomed destiny as a roadside mechanic were woven into a single breath!

And how did our beloved Raju respond to this terrifying tempest?

If R.K. Narayan's immortal 'Swami' had stepped out of the pages of Malgudi Days and into our house, he would have looked identical to Raju in that moment.

Raju stood casually against the whitewashed veranda pillar. He gently rubbed the dust from his left calf with the big toe of his right foot. His large, round eyes blinked with the serene, unbothered innocence of a meditating monk. There was no terror on his countenance, no tears, no stuttered excuses. He was simply waiting with superhuman patience for Mother's verbal locomotive to run out of steam.

And at the ten-minute mark, when Mother finally paused for two solitary seconds to draw oxygen into her depleted lungs...

Raju wiped the sweat from his grimy brow with the back of his dusty forearm. He looked Mother squarely in the eyes. Then he tilted his head, peered past her shoulder directly toward the simmering pots on the kitchen stove, and asked in the sweetest, most innocent, famished voice ever recorded in human history:

The Immortal Question

"Mummy, what is for lunch today? I am terribly hungry. Could you dish out some rice quickly, please?"

Silence. A silence so profound that you could hear the wall clock ticking in the next room!

Mother's wooden rolling pin froze mid-air. Her eyes expanded until they threatened to dislodge from their sockets. She stared at Raju as though an extraterrestrial had landed on her veranda, devoured fifty rupees and three hours of time, and was now graciously inquiring about the luncheon menu!

Beside her, my sister and I bit down on our lower lips with all the willpower in our teenage bodies. We squeezed each other's hands until our knuckles turned white. But the sight of Raju's dusty face, the taped bat slung over his shoulder, and that magnificent, unblemished lack of guilt...

Suddenly, a choked snort escaped my sister's nose—and the volcano erupted!

We collapsed onto the veranda floor, clutching our aching stomachs. Tears of sheer, hysterical laughter streamed down our faces. We laughed until our ribs hurt, until we were gasping for air against the wall. Mother tried valiantly to maintain her scowl for another three seconds, but looking down at her two daughters rolling on the floor and Raju standing there with his hopeful empty plate... Mother's lips twitched, her stern mask shattered, and her own laughter rang out across the courtyard!

Scene 5: The Durrie Feast & The Final Audacity

Needless to say, not a single samosa graced our plates that Sunday.

In our home, dining inside the kitchen was strictly forbidden. A handwoven blue-and-white cotton durrie was unrolled across the cool mosaic floor of the living room, and the entire family sat cross-legged together in a circle.

Stainless steel thalis were laid out, filled with piping-hot yellow arhar dal, steamed white rice, and quarters of raw onion. Having starved since dawn, that humble dal-rice tasted more sumptuous than an emperor's banquet.

Yet our 'Swami' had one final, immortal card left to play.

Raju wolfed down three colossal handfuls of dal-rice like a castaway rescued from a deserted island. Then, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand, he looked down at the remaining yellow dal, wrinkled his nose in mild disappointment, and voiced his genuine grievance to Mother:

The Audacity of the Century

"Wait a minute! Today was Sunday... so Mummy, why didn't you cook Rajma-Chawal?!"

The steel thalis rattled as our jaws dropped to the durrie!

My sister instantly raised her steel katori like a projectile weapon, glared at him with mock vengeance, and snapped:

"Eat what you have been given and shut up! Given your stunts today, the only thing you deserved to eat was Mummy's slippers!"

Once again, the room dissolved into helpless laughter. Raju cleaned his plate down to the last grain, drank an entire brass lota of water, and produced a long, contented burp that signalled his complete satisfaction with the day's achievements.

“Thirty years later, I realize that had Raju brought those samosas home on time, we would have eaten them in twenty minutes and forgotten all about them. But by losing them, he gifted our family a story that has brought us tears of laughter for three decades!”

Today in 2026, I live in a world of supreme digital convenience. With a single tap on my smartphone, I can summon gourmet samosas, artisanal pizzas, or authentic rajma-chawal from the finest restaurants in the city. A tiny digital motorcycle glides across my screen, updating its GPS coordinates every four seconds.

Yet where is the soul in that efficiency?

Where is the dusty afternoon sunlight of the 1990s? Where is the yellow electrical tape on a split cricket bat? Where is the lost fifty-rupee note, the cold pot of ginger tea with its wrinkled skin, and the breathtaking innocence of a brother who could turn family starvation into a legendary comedy? Whenever this memory resurfaces during my evening cup of chai, a wide, unstoppable grin spreads across my face. That screen-free, unhurried, beautifully imperfect 90s childhood was truly a treasure beyond compare.

Did you also have a 'Raju' in your family—a sibling dispatched on a critical mission who returned hours later, empty-handed, covered in dust, and completely oblivious to their crimes? Share your 90s memories in the comments below—because revisiting these golden days brings their warmth alive in our hearts all over again!
