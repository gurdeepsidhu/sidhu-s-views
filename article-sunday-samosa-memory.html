The Great Sunday Samosa Incident of the 90s

A sweet, hilarious memory of 1990s childhood: when there were no mobile phones, fast food wasn't a daily trend, and an unbothered brother forgot the samosas and innocently asked for lunch.

By Gurdeep Sidhu • September 20, 2026 • 9 min read • 90s Nostalgia
Language: English | 🇮🇳 हिंदी (Hinglish) →

  2. Scene 2: The Agonizing Two-Hour Wait & Forced Kitchen Duty (10:00 AM - 12:00 PM)
  3. Scene 3: The Grand Arrival & The Empty Hands (12:30 PM)
  4. Scene 4: Mother's Thunder & The Legendary Question
  5. Scene 5: Why Lost Samosas Become the Sweetest 90s Memories

Back in the 1990s, childhood in an Indian household had a completely different rhythm—unhurried, sweet, and wonderfully simple. There were no smartphones glued to our palms, no endless algorithmic feeds, and no social media clamoring for attention. Sunday mornings began with the warm, nostalgic strains of Doordarshan's Rangoli at eight o'clock. We siblings genuinely spent our entire days together—sprawling on the cool veranda floor, chatting for hours, bickering over carrom or ludo, and enjoying simple everyday moments.

And in those 90s days, fast food was neither a trend nor a daily lifestyle. There were no food delivery apps where you tapped a screen and had hot meals arrive on your doorstep in ten minutes. In our home, Mother lived by an unshakeable, golden rule: "Freshly cooked dal-roti is what keeps you healthy; food from outside is never eaten every day!" Getting outside food—especially hot, golden, crispy samosas straight from the halwai's smoking iron cauldron—was never an ordinary habit or a fixed weekly routine. It was an extremely rare, almost royal celebration that happened once in many blue moons.

Precisely because it happened so rarely, on the occasional Sunday morning when Mother finally consented to sending for samosas, our excitement hit the absolute ceiling! The entire house buzzed with an electric current of pure anticipation. That Sunday, the air was fragrant from the very start. Mother was in the kitchen, boiling a generous pot of strong ginger and crushed cardamom tea on the stove. Meanwhile, my sister and I were perched right by the veranda window with mouths watering, practically vibrating with impatience, counting down every single second.

"In the 90s, outside snacks weren't a routine—they were a rare festival. And when a missing bag of samosas tested that excitement, our family drama reached peak comedy."

Scene 1: The Rare 90s Sunday Morning Mission (9:00 AM)

The wall clock struck exactly nine. The spicy, comforting aroma of brewing ginger tea filled the hallway. Mother summoned my thirteen-year-old younger brother, Raju, into the kitchen.

In those days, entrusting a thirteen-year-old with family money for a market errand was a monumental responsibility. Mother produced a crisp, fresh fifty-rupee note. Back in the nineties, fifty rupees was serious money—a king's ransom in the eyes of a child. Pressing the note firmly into Raju's palm, Mother looked him straight in the eyes and delivered her strict, no-nonsense briefing:

"Listen to me very carefully, Raju," Mother instructed in a solemn tone. "Go straight to the halwai shop. Don't look left, don't look right. Tell him to pack eight piping-hot, crispy samosas. Make sure he packs both the spicy green mint chutney and the sweet tamarind saunth. Do not get distracted by friends, do not stop anywhere to play, and come straight back through that gate in exactly ten minutes!"

To be completely honest, in our household, sending Raju on any market errand was always a high-stakes gamble. This wasn't his first stunt—it was practically his trademark routine! Whenever he was sent out to fetch something, whether it was a packet of bread, milk, or fresh coriander, he would invariably get seduced by a roadside game of marbles or cricket and forget the entire purpose of his mission. That was precisely why Mother had laid down such strict, non-negotiable ground rules this time.

Raju nodded with profound, stone-faced seriousness, looking for all the world like an elite secret agent entrusted with national security. He stuffed the fifty-rupee note deep into his pocket and marched toward the door.

My sister and I stood by the front gate, waving him off as though he were an astronaut boarding a spacecraft. My sister yelled after him down the path: "Raju! Get the freshest, super-crispy ones! And hold the chutney bag carefully so it doesn't burst on the way!" Raju grabbed his bicycle, rang the bell with a cheery 'trin-trin', gave us a resolute thumbs-up, and pedaled rapidly down the sunlit street.

Scene 2: The Agonizing Two-Hour Wait & Forced Kitchen Duty (10:00 AM - 12:00 PM)

Ten minutes passed... Fifteen minutes passed... Twenty minutes ticked by.

Half an hour vanished into the quiet Sunday morning air, yet the street remained completely empty. There was no sign of Raju or his bicycle.

Remember, this was the 1990s—there were no mobile phones! You couldn't call his cell, there was no landline phone at the halwai shop, and the concept of GPS live tracking didn't exist even in science fiction. At home, we could only make guesses. At first, we assumed the halwai was simply swamped with the usual Sunday morning rush of the entire neighbourhood. Or perhaps the first batch had completely sold out and the halwai was patiently frying a fresh lot in his massive iron kadhai.

By 10:30 AM, however, our patience was beginning to unravel. In the kitchen, the pot of ginger tea had grown stone cold, with a thick, wrinkled skin of cream setting across its surface. In our stomachs, hunger was staging a full-scale mutiny. My sister and I abandoned the window and slumped onto the veranda steps, starving and miserable, glaring down the deserted road like desert travellers praying for rain.

By 11:30 AM, the temperature and tension inside the house reached boiling point. Mother was pacing relentlessly between the veranda and the front gate, checking the round wall clock every thirty seconds. Her face was a storm of deep bewilderment and rising irritation:

Mounting Fury & A Starving Household

"Where on earth has Raju gotten stuck?" Mother muttered, peering anxiously out into the glaring street. "The halwai is barely a five-minute bicycle ride away! The tea is completely ruined, and it's already midday... Breakfast is gone, and it's practically time to start cooking lunch!"

My sister had turned beet-red from a combustible mix of hunger and exasperation. She stamped her foot hard on the veranda step and let out a furious growl:

"Just let him step through that gate today... Just let his feet touch the threshold! You wait and watch the thrashing he gets from Mummy! My stomach is rumbling so loud it hurts, I'm starving, and I am boiling mad! I bet my life he got roped into playing games with his friends somewhere!"

And our misery didn't stop there! At twelve o'clock sharp, Mother officially surrendered all hope of breakfast and made a grim announcement: "Come on, both of you. Forget the samosas and get into the kitchen to help me cook lunch!"

Our plight was comical. We had been starving since nine in the morning with mouths watering for crispy samosas, and now we were conscripted into kitchen forced labour! Neither of us had the slightest desire to work. Tears were streaming down our faces from chopping pungent onions, rolling pins and lentil pots felt like ten-ton weights, and every ounce of our mounting frustration was directed squarely at Raju: 'Just let that boy come home, we'll show him!'

Scene 3: The Grand Arrival & The Empty Hands (12:30 PM)

The clock struck 12:30 PM. High noon was blazing overhead. Amidst stinging onion tears, ferocious hunger, and boiling tempers, an ominous silence settled over our home.

Suddenly, breaking through the quiet lane, a cheerful, careless whistle echoed outside!

We snapped our heads toward the window—and there, leaning his bicycle against the outside wall, was Raju!

His entrance was an absolute theatrical spectacle. He strolled through the gate with the serene, unruffled composure of a king returning from a leisurely royal stroll. Both his knees were heavily caked in dry grey dust and mud. His shirt collar was unbuttoned, his hair stuck out in all directions like a crow's nest after a cyclone, and sweat was trickling down his dusty temples. In his right hand, swinging nonchalantly by his thigh, was a cracked, battered wooden cricket bat.

And in his left hand? NOTHING. ABSOLUTELY NOTHING.

No newspaper parcel. No paper bag. Not a solitary samosa, and not a single microscopic drop of green chutney! The crisp fifty-rupee note had also vanished into thin air.

In that single, crystal-clear moment, the entire three-hour mystery solved itself: the neighbourhood boys had called out to him on his way to play 'just one quick over.' And Raju had plunged into that gully cricket battle so deeply that time, fifty rupees, hot samosas, breakfast, and his starving family had been completely wiped clean from his memory!

The second he crossed the doorway, my sister and I stormed out of the kitchen. We planted our feet, crossed our arms, and glared at him from head to toe as if ready to pulverize him on the spot! If human eyes could emit heat, that cracked cricket bat in his hand would have burst into flames right then and there.

Scene 4: Mother's Thunder & The Legendary Question

Before my sister or I could utter a single word, Mother strode out from the kitchen like a thunderstorm. Taking in his dusty knees, ruffled hair, battered bat, and completely empty hands, her fury detonated across the veranda:

"RAJU! Where are the samosas?! Where is the fifty-rupee note?! Have you seen the clock—it is half past twelve! The entire house has been sitting here starving since nine in the morning waiting for you! Have you been playing cricket in the dirt with your friends all this time while we starve?!"

Mother unleashed ten continuous minutes of relentless Indian motherly wrath—a volcanic eruption combining three and a half hours of worry, hunger, wasted tea, and pure maternal outrage.

And what was Raju's response to this non-stop ten-minute dressing-down?

He stood quietly against the wall, perfectly still. He blinked his large, round eyes with the serene innocence of a startled deer. There was no terror on his face, no tears pooling in his eyes, and not even the slightest attempt to spin a frantic excuse. He simply waited with sublime patience, allowing Mother's verbal locomotive to run its course until she had to pause for breath.

And the very instant Mother paused after ten minutes to draw a single breath...

Raju casually wiped the sweat from his dusty brow with the back of his arm. He looked Mother straight in the eye, glanced casually past her toward the kitchen, and asked in the sweetest, calmest, most innocent voice imaginable:

The Legendary Question

"Mummy, what is in the lunch today? I am very hungry. Can you serve me rice quickly?"

The silence that descended upon the veranda following that question was so heavy you could hear the ceiling fan humming.

Nobody suddenly became normal, and nobody rushed forward with a warm smile to serve him food. The truth was, Mother stood there glaring at him with wide, disbelieving eyes, her face screaming: 'You lost fifty rupees, you played cricket in the dirt for three hours, you starved your family, and now you have the nerve to ask for rice like an honoured royal guest?!'

Meanwhile, my sister and I were doing everything in our power to maintain our fearsome, stone-faced glares. But taking in Raju's dusty, sweat-streaked, innocent face, his battered bat, and the breathtaking audacity of asking for lunch with zero guilt...

Both of us sisters burst into helpless, uncontrollable hysterics!

My sister clamped a hand over her mouth, but a loud snort escaped. Within two seconds, both of us collapsed against the veranda wall, clutching our aching stomachs, laughing so hard that tears were streaming down our cheeks. Mother tried her absolute best to keep her stern scowl in place, but watching her two daughters laughing like maniacs on the floor, while Raju stood there still innocently expecting his plate of rice, her composure cracked. A smile broke across her lips—and within moments, the entire house was echoing with warm, helpless laughter!

Scene 5: Why Lost Samosas Become the Sweetest Memories

Needless to say, we never tasted a single samosa that Sunday. In our home, we never ate inside the kitchen—instead, a cotton durrie was spread across the floor, and we all sat together on the durrie enjoying simple, piping-hot dal and rice.

Yet Raju's Swami-like innocence and sublime audacity were far from over! He looked down at the plate of simple dal and rice, pulled a disappointed face, and casually blurted out:

The Classic Audacity

"What! You didn't make Rajma-Chawal today?!"

The moment those words left his mouth, all three of us—Mother, my sister, and I—froze in sheer, absolute disbelief! We turned simultaneously and drilled him with lethal glares that silently screamed: 'That's it, he's a dead man now!' A boy who lost fifty rupees, forgot the samosas, played cricket for three hours, and starved his family had the sheer gall to demand Rajma-Chawal!

I immediately waved my hand in front of his plate, eyes wide with indignation, and snapped: "Just eat quietly whatever is served, before you end up getting beaten with a slipper!"

Hearing that, all of us burst into helpless laughter once again. Raju merely grinned sheepishly, cleaned his plate, and looked thoroughly satisfied with himself.

Looking back decades later at those 1990s childhood days, I realize that those lost samosas were never just about breakfast. They were a precious artifact of a simpler, screen-free world—an era when treats were rare and cherished, where sibling bonds were forged in gully dust and laughter, and where a little brother's innocent question could transform a household crisis into a story that brings smiles to our faces for a lifetime.

Even today, decades later, whenever this memory crosses my mind—whether in the middle of a busy workday or while quietly sipping my evening tea—a wide smile involuntarily spreads across my face, followed by an irrepressible laugh. Truth be told, that 1990s screen-free childhood was infinitely more magical, soulful, and beautiful than today's hyper-connected, digital world. Seriously, it was such an innocent, deeply precious era that can never truly be replaced.

Did something similar ever happen in your own childhood too? Did you also have a brother or sister who was sent on a simple errand, only to lose all track of time, forget everything, and return hours later with dusty clothes and completely empty hands? Share your own childhood stories with me—because revisiting these golden memories brings the magic of the 90s alive all over again!
